1. Transportation Cost Minimization

A retailer ships a product from two warehouses to two stores. Each warehouse has a limited supply, each store has a minimum demand, and cost per unit differs by route. Goal: meet every store's demand at the lowest possible total cost.

Model Formulation

Minimize  Z = 2xW1,S1 + 3xW1,S2 + 1xW2,S1 + 4xW2,S2
ConstraintExpressionMeaning
Warehouse 1 supplyxW1,S1 + xW1,S2 ≤ 100Cannot ship more than 100 units from W1
Warehouse 2 supplyxW2,S1 + xW2,S2 ≤ 80Cannot ship more than 80 units from W2
Store 1 demandxW1,S1 + xW2,S1 ≥ 60Store 1 needs at least 60 units
Store 2 demandxW1,S2 + xW2,S2 ≥ 70Store 2 needs at least 70 units

Optimal Shipping Plan

RouteUnit CostUnits ShippedRoute Cost
Warehouse 1 → Store 1$20$0
Warehouse 1 → Store 2$370$210
Warehouse 2 → Store 1$160$60
Warehouse 2 → Store 2$40$0
Total$270

Business Interpretation

The solver saturates the cheapest routes first — W1→S2 ($3/unit) and W2→S1 ($1/unit) — and leaves the pricier W1→S1 and W2→S2 routes unused entirely. Both warehouses still have spare capacity (30 units at W1, 20 at W2), which tells us demand, not supply, is what's limiting this network — a very different lever to pull than "buy more warehouse capacity."

2. Investment Portfolio Allocation

A private investor has $100,000 to split between two stocks and wants to maximize expected return without exceeding a personal risk tolerance — a classic risk/return trade-off that LP resolves exactly rather than "on balance."

Model Formulation

Maximize  Z = 0.15xA + 0.10xB
ConstraintExpressionMeaning
BudgetxA + xB ≤ 100Cannot invest more than $100,000 total ($k)
Risk budget0.5xA + 0.2xB ≤ 30Weighted risk exposure capped at 30 units

Optimal Allocation

AssetReturnInvestmentShare
Stock A15%$33,33333.3%
Stock B10%$66,66766.7%
Expected Return$11,667

Business Interpretation

Both the budget and risk constraints are exactly binding at the optimum (100% of each is used) — the geometric signature of an LP solution, which always sits at a vertex where two or more constraints meet. Stock A returns more, but burns through the risk budget over twice as fast (0.5 vs. 0.2 risk units per dollar), so the optimal mix leans on the more risk-efficient Stock B to put the remaining capital to work rather than leaving it idle.

3. Production Mix Optimization

A custom furniture workshop makes Desks and Chairs and needs to decide how many of each to build each week to maximize profit, given limited labor hours and limited wood supply.

Model Formulation

Maximize  Z = 40x + 30y
ConstraintExpressionMeaning
Labor2x + y ≤ 100100 labor-hours available; desks need 2 hrs, chairs need 1 hr
Woodx + 3y ≤ 9090 board-ft available; desks need 1 ft, chairs need 3 ft

Optimal Production Plan

ProductProfit / UnitUnits / WeekProfit Contribution
Desks$4042$1,680
Chairs$3016$480
Weekly Profit$2,160

Business Interpretation

The optimal plan uses 100% of both labor and wood — both constraints bind at once, so growing profit further means relaxing one of them, not just working harder. Desks are far more wood-efficient ($40 profit/board-ft vs. $10 for chairs), while chairs are more labor-efficient ($30/hour vs. $20/hour for desks). Rather than picking a single "best" product, the LP finds the exact mix that saturates both scarce resources simultaneously.

Key Takeaways

This case study demonstrates an end-to-end optimization workflow that generalizes to any resource-allocation problem an analytics team might face — logistics networks, portfolios, or production schedules of any size.

  • Interpreting results in business terms — not just reporting numbers
  • Sensitivity analysis (shadow prices) to quantify what relaxing a constraint is actually worth
  • Visualizing feasible regions and trade-offs for non-technical stakeholders
